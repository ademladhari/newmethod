\chapter*{Abstract}

The rapid proliferation of AI-generated imagery, driven primarily by latent diffusion architectures such as Stable Diffusion, DALL-E, and Midjourney, has created a pressing need for reliable mechanisms of content provenance. This thesis investigates imperceptible digital watermarking as a technical response to that problem. I conduct a systematic literature survey and comparative evaluation of thirty-one watermarking algorithms, spanning classical frequency-domain transforms (DWT, DCT, SVD and their hybrids), deep learning encoder-decoder frameworks (HiDDeN, RivaGAN, StegaStamp), latent-space methods, and techniques designed to integrate watermarking directly into the diffusion generation process (Tree-Ring, The Stable Signature, Gaussian Shading, PTW, ZoDiac, and others). Selected algorithms are implemented across multiple generative AI environments and evaluated on a unified benchmark covering imperceptibility metrics (PSNR, SSIM), robustness to a defined suite of image attacks (JPEG compression, geometric transforms, noise, regeneration), and detection reliability (bit accuracy, AUC). I find that pipeline-integrated methods (those that embed the watermark during generation rather than post-hoc) offer substantially higher robustness against regeneration attacks, at the cost of requiring control over the generation model itself. Classical transform methods remain competitive in low-attack scenarios but degrade rapidly under modern compression pipelines. No single method dominates all evaluation axes simultaneously. The thesis concludes with a structured taxonomy of the methods, a practical recommendation framework, and identified directions for future research.

  \begin{flushleft}
    Keywords: digital watermarking, generative AI, latent diffusion models, image authentication, steganography, perceptual robustness
    \end{flushleft}

\section{Background and Motivation}

Something changed in 2022, and it changed fast. Stable Diffusion 1.4 was released publicly in August of that year \cite{rombach2022ldm}. DALL-E 2 had arrived a few months earlier, in April \cite{ramesh2022dalle2}. Midjourney v3, which was already producing results that confused professional photographers in online blind tests, launched that same summer \cite{holz2022midjourney}. Within eighteen months of those releases, high-quality generative image tools were running locally on consumer hardware. The images stopped looking artificial. They started looking real: not approximately real, but perceptually indistinguishable from photographs in controlled studies \cite{nightingale2022faces}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{download.png}
    \caption{AI generation improvement (illustrative example). Source: Stanford Institute for Human-Centered Artificial Intelligence, \textit{AI Index Report 2024}, Figure~2.4.2, 2024~\cite{aiindex2024newatlas}.}
    \label{fig:ai-generation-improvement}
\end{figure}

The problem is not the images themselves. The problem is that they carry no intrinsic trace of their origin. A photograph taken with a Nikon D850 embeds EXIF metadata automatically: camera model, GPS coordinates, timestamp, lens, and focal length, all of which can be used to identify the origin and validate ownership \cite{exif2019spec}. That metadata can be stripped, but its absence is itself a signal. AI-generated images have no equivalent. A synthetically produced portrait inserted into a news article is, at the pixel level, identical to a real photograph. No forensic tool can recover what was never there to begin with, and the scale at which these images are being produced --- billions per month across the major platforms by 2024 \cite{scale_citation} --- makes manual review impossible.

Watermarking is the attempt to put something back. The term comes from papermaking, where semi-transparent patterns were pressed into the pulp during manufacture to indicate provenance and deter counterfeiting \cite{hunter1957papermaking}. Digital watermarking has existed as a formal research area since at least the early 1990s, with \cite{cox1997}'s 1997 paper on secure spread-spectrum watermarking establishing much of the vocabulary still in use. What has changed, radically, is the context: the threat model, the scale, and the capabilities of both the embedding side and the attack side \cite{cox2007book}.

The EU AI Act, adopted in March 2024, includes provisions requiring that AI-generated content be marked as such \cite{euaiact2024}. The US Executive Order on AI, signed in October 2023, directed federal agencies to develop standards for watermarking AI content \cite{biden2023eo}. NIST published preliminary guidelines in early 2024 \cite{nist2024synthetic}. The policy momentum is clear. The technical capability to fulfil those policies is, I will argue, still catching up.

This thesis is about that gap --- and about one architectural direction for narrowing it.

\section{Problem Statement}
 
Learned encoder--decoder watermarking systems such as HiDDeN \cite{zhu2018} train a single monolithic decoder to recover a hidden payload from images that have been subjected to a variety of distortions. The decoder is optimised against the average loss across all attack types in the training distribution: JPEG compression, additive noise, geometric transforms, and their combinations. This averaging is a structural compromise. JPEG compression corrupts the watermark through frequency-domain quantisation; additive noise corrupts it through unstructured signal interference; geometric transforms corrupt it through spatial misalignment. These are structurally different problems, and there is no a priori reason that a single set of decoder weights should be equally well-suited to recovering the mark from all of them. By training a monolithic decoder to handle everything, no component of the network can specialise on the recovery mechanism most appropriate for any particular distortion type.
 
A mixture-of-experts (MoE) formulation is a natural response to this heterogeneity. Rather than committing all decoding capacity to a single set of weights, an MoE decoder partitions the input space across specialised sub-networks and routes each query to the expert best suited to handle it. This principle has driven significant performance gains in large language models \cite{shazeer2017}, vision transformers \cite{riquelme2021}, and multi-task learning \cite{ma2018}, but it has not previously been applied to the image watermarking problem.
 
This thesis investigates whether a MoE decoder architecture, trained on top of the HiDDeN encoder--decoder backbone \cite{zhu2018}, can improve robustness across a heterogeneous attack distribution relative to a single-decoder baseline, and characterises the training conditions under which the architecture succeeds or fails.
 
\section{Research Objectives and Questions}
 
The primary objective of this thesis is to design, implement, and evaluate a mixture-of-experts watermarking decoder, investigating whether learned sparse routing over specialised sub-networks can improve robustness across a heterogeneous set of transformation attacks relative to the HiDDeN baseline it extends. A secondary objective is to characterise the training dynamics of the MoE architecture --- specifically the conditions under which routing generalises and the failure modes that arise when it does not.
 
These objectives give rise to the following research questions:
 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Does introducing a MoE decoder degrade the fundamental clean-channel watermarking capability of the HiDDeN backbone, and does routing become active and generalisable within the training budget?
    \item How does the MoE decoder compare to the HiDDeN baseline on bit error rate across a heterogeneous set of standard image distortions, under a clean-trained evaluation where neither model was exposed to attacks during training?
    \item Which architectural and hyperparameter choices --- backbone freezing, routing sparsity $k$, expert count $N$, and training duration --- determine whether the MoE architecture converges to a stable, generalisable routing partition, and what failure signatures characterise each wrong choice?
    \item What is the inference-time computational overhead of the MoE decoder relative to HiDDeN at batch sizes relevant to deployment, and how does the overhead amortise with batch size?
    \item Does the routing collapse score reliably predict final decoding performance across training runs, and can distinct failure modes be identified and diagnosed from routing diagnostics alone?
\end{enumerate}
 
\section{Scope and Limitations}
 
This thesis focuses exclusively on still images. Video, text, and audio watermarking are outside scope entirely.
 
The MoE architecture is built on top of the HiDDeN backbone \cite{zhu2018}. HiDDeN serves as the primary baseline given that the MoE decoder directly extends it, allowing the contribution of the routing layer to be isolated by holding the rest of the architecture constant. The evaluation also includes a representative set of existing watermarking methods to situate the MoE results within the broader landscape; these are described in Chapter~2 and evaluated in Chapter~4.
 
The evaluation uses $128 \times 128$ image crops from COCO-2017, a 30-bit payload, and a fixed set of standard image distortions: JPEG compression, Gaussian noise, crop, cropout, resize, dropout, quantisation, and their combination. Neither model was attack-trained; the evaluation tests whether routing learned on clean data provides generalisation advantages under standard distortions applied only at test time. The reported numbers reflect this specific experimental setting and should not be read as deployment performance claims.
 
\section{Thesis Contribution}
 
The primary contributions of this thesis are as follows.
 
First, a novel mixture-of-experts watermarking architecture, in which four specialised decoder sub-networks are trained to recover watermark payloads from distorted images, with a learned sparse top-1 routing mechanism assigning each input to a single expert at inference time. To the author's knowledge, this is the first application of a MoE formulation to the image watermarking problem.
 
Second, an empirical evaluation of this architecture against the HiDDeN baseline across nine distortion conditions, establishing where sparse routing provides robustness gains, where it does not, and why the outcomes differ by distortion type.
 
Third, a characterisation of the training conditions under which MoE routing succeeds or fails, including the identification of three distinct failure modes --- full routing collapse, partial routing collapse, and train--validation routing mismatch --- and a two-metric diagnostic protocol (\texttt{max\_use} and \texttt{load\_l1}) for detecting each one during training.
 
Fourth, an inference cost analysis establishing the computational overhead of the MoE decoder relative to HiDDeN at batch sizes 1, 8, and 32, demonstrating that the batch-1 dispatch overhead amortises substantially by batch 32.
 

\section{Thesis Structure}

Chapter~2 reviews the literature, covering the most important methods across technical generations and concluding with a discussion of mixture-of-experts architectures and their relevance to the watermarking problem. Chapter~3 describes the dataset, implementation environment, and evaluation design. Chapter~4 presents experimental results across five research questions. Chapter~5 discusses what those results mean and where they fall short. Chapter~6 concludes with practical recommendations and directions for future work.

\section{Evaluation Framework}

Before surveying individual methods, it is necessary to establish the evaluative vocabulary the field has converged on. Every claim of ``robustness'' or ``imperceptibility'' is only meaningful relative to the metrics and attack protocols against which it is assessed.

\subsection*{Image Quality Metrics}

The two foundational perceptual metrics are Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR) and the Structural Similarity Index Measure (SSIM). PSNR expresses the ratio between maximum possible pixel energy and the distortion energy introduced by watermarking, in decibels; values above 35~dB are conventionally considered imperceptible \cite{huynhthu2008}. SSIM, introduced by \cite{wang2004}, departs from pixel-error framing entirely: human visual perception is adapted for extracting structural information from scenes, so a quality metric should measure degradation of structure rather than deviation of pixel values. SSIM assesses luminance, contrast, and structure separately, with values above 0.98 indicating perceptually transparent embedding. For diffusion-era methods operating at the distribution level, a third metric is necessary. Fr\'{e}chet Inception Distance (FID), introduced by \cite{heusel2017}, captures whether embedding shifts the statistical distribution of images in ways invisible to pixel-level metrics but detectable in feature space. Beyond these, robustness evaluations standardly report bit accuracy for multi-bit encoders, True Positive Rate at fixed False Positive Rate (TPR@FPR), and Normalised Correlation (NC) for classical methods.

\subsection*{Attack Benchmarking}

The attack taxonomy situation was poorly standardised before 2024. Each paper evaluated robustness against its own hand-picked attack set, making cross-method comparisons unreliable. \cite{an2024} addressed this with WAVES (Watermark Analysis Via Enhanced Stress-testing), published at ICML 2024, which integrates detection and identification tasks under a standardised protocol spanning traditional image distortions and advanced regeneration variants. What makes WAVES significant is its treatment of regeneration: prior work considered only single-pass regeneration, while WAVES introduces rinsing regenerations --- multiple cycles of noising and denoising --- plus prompted and mixed variants. Even methods previously considered robust do not survive these conditions. The attack suite used in this thesis is drawn from WAVES.

\subsection*{The Regeneration Threat}

The regeneration attack was formalised theoretically by \cite{zhao2023}, who showed that invisible pixel-level watermarks are provably removable by generative AI. The information-theoretic argument is clean: a diffusion model's reconstruction marginalises over the degrees of freedom occupied by the watermark signal, because those degrees of freedom were not part of the semantic content used to condition the denoiser. The watermark lives in a subspace the model does not care about preserving. This result renders the entire prior threat model --- JPEG compression, additive noise, geometric distortions --- insufficient for post-hoc methods, and motivates every diffusion-native approach discussed below.

\section{Classical Frequency-Domain Watermarking}

The first generation of digital watermarking methods, dominant through the early 2000s, embedded marks by modifying coefficients in frequency-domain transform spaces. \cite{cox1997} established the foundational framework with spread-spectrum watermarking, embedding a pseudo-random sequence into the perceptually significant mid-frequency DCT coefficients of the luminance channel. The spread-spectrum approach distributes the watermark energy across many coefficients rather than concentrating it in a few, making localised removal difficult without substantial image degradation. Detection is performed by correlating the extracted coefficients with the known key sequence. \cite{cox1997}'s method achieves reasonable robustness under mild JPEG compression and additive noise, and its mathematical clarity made it the reference point against which subsequent methods defined themselves.

The hybrid DWT-DCT-SVD approach, developed through a series of works in the mid-2000s \cite{cox2007book}, extended this framework by combining three transforms in sequence: a discrete wavelet transform to decompose the image into frequency subbands, a DCT applied within each subband, and singular value decomposition to embed the payload in the most energetically significant components. The cascade provides layered redundancy: the DWT separates frequency information spatially, the DCT exploits local frequency structure, and the SVD targets the most perceptually stable image components. In practice this yields better imperceptibility at comparable robustness relative to single-transform methods. However, SVD-based extraction carries a well-documented security flaw noted by \cite{cox2007book}: the extraction procedure depends on the singular vectors of any decomposition rather than a secret key, making the scheme susceptible to inversion attacks in which an adversary uses a watermarked image to fraudulently claim ownership of an unmarked one.

Both approaches fail against modern attack suites. Against JPEG-Q50 compression or even moderate geometric transforms, most frequency-domain methods degrade rapidly. They were designed for a world in which the primary threat was casual copying, not adversarial manipulation by a second generative model.

\section{Deep Learning Watermarking}

\subsection*{The Encoder--Decoder Paradigm}

The second generation replaced fixed frequency-domain transforms with learned ones, enabling joint optimisation of imperceptibility and robustness that classical methods could not achieve. \cite{zhu2018} introduced HiDDeN: an encoder hides an $N$-bit message inside a cover image, a paired decoder recovers it, and an adversarial discriminator enforces perceptual fidelity, with a differentiable noise layer simulating JPEG, cropping, and Gaussian noise during training. The key insight is that the entire pipeline --- encoder, noise layer, decoder --- is differentiable, so the model learns to embed in precisely those image statistics that survive the attack distribution it was trained against. HiDDeN achieves near-perfect bit accuracy under moderate compression at PSNR above 33~dB for a 30-bit payload, and the encoder--decoder training paradigm it introduced became the template for the methods that followed.

The noise layer in HiDDeN also revealed a fundamental limitation of the approach. The robustness of a learned encoder--decoder system is bounded by the attack distribution used during training: train against JPEG and crop, get robustness against JPEG and crop. Nothing else is guaranteed. This observation motivates the robust optimisation framing of ROMark \cite{wen2019}, which minimises worst-case loss across multiple attack types rather than average-case loss, framing robustness as a minimax problem and yielding quantified rather than purely empirical guarantees. \cite{tancik2020} extended HiDDeN to the physical domain with StegaStamp, training with perspective warps, colour shifts, and moir\'{e} simulation to survive camera re-capture of printed outputs, achieving bit error rates below 0.01 from smartphone photographs at PSNR 31--34~dB.

HiDDeN serves as the backbone of the MoE architecture developed in this thesis, motivating a more detailed treatment than the other methods reviewed here. Its encoder is a U-Net with skip connections that learns to distribute the payload signal across the image in a content-adaptive manner; its decoder mirrors this architecture to recover the payload from a distorted watermarked image. The adversarial discriminator provides an implicit perceptual regulariser that prevents the encoder from exploiting frequency channels that are perceptually conspicuous. The primary weakness of this and related encoder--decoder methods is exactly the regeneration vulnerability formalised by \cite{zhao2023}: because the watermark is applied to an already-generated image, it occupies degrees of freedom the generative model does not need to preserve during reconstruction.

\subsection*{Latent Space Methods}

\cite{fernandez2022} reframed the embedding problem at the representation level with the SSL Watermark, embedding a watermark direction into the latent space of a frozen self-supervised DINO model via lightweight fine-tuning, and detecting by checking whether a query image's embedding falls within the marked subspace. The geometric view of watermarking --- the embedding as a direction in a learned representation space rather than a pixel-level perturbation --- directly anticipated the diffusion-native methods that followed. PSNR above 42~dB and AUC above 0.99 without per-image optimisation at inference time represent the ceiling of the post-hoc paradigm under benign conditions. Under regeneration attacks, performance degrades for the same structural reason as HiDDeN: the watermark occupies a subspace in the image that a diffusion model's reconstruction does not preserve.

\section{Diffusion-Native Watermarking}

The regeneration attack established a new design imperative. If superimposing a watermark on generated output means the mark lives in degrees of freedom the generative model discards during reconstruction, then the mark must be entangled with the generative process itself. The literature responds across two primary strategies: injecting structure into the initial noise vector, and fine-tuning model weights.

\subsection*{Initial-Noise Injection}

Tree-Ring Watermarks \cite{wen2023} embed the mark in the initial noise vector by imposing a ring pattern in its Fourier domain, exploiting the near-determinism of DDIM sampling to make the pattern recoverable by diffusion inversion. Because the watermark is placed in the noise from which the image is generated, rather than added to the image afterwards, it is substantially more robust to regeneration: a second generative model reconstructing the image semantically does not touch the noise-space structure from which the original was drawn. Tree-Ring requires no model modification and reports AUC above 0.99 under JPEG and brightness attacks at zero inference overhead. Its limitation, identified by \cite{ci2024} in a systematic re-examination, is that the ring pattern cannot distinguish between different watermark keys: in a multi-user deployment, the method can detect a watermark but not identify whose it is.

Gaussian Shading \cite{yang2024} replaced the ring structure with something statistically principled: the watermark is mapped to a latent representation provably indistinguishable from the standard Gaussian prior, achieving distribution-lossless embedding by construction and eliminating FID degradation entirely. The method applies near-optimal soft-decision decoding by treating the full generation and inversion pipeline as an AWGN channel, substantially improving robustness under guidance-scale mismatch between embedding and detection time. The PRC Watermark \cite{gunn2024} takes the most cryptographically rigorous position on noise injection, selecting the initial noise using a pseudorandom error-correcting code derived from a secret key and providing formal soundness guarantees: an adversary without the key cannot construct a valid detection claim even with access to many watermarked images, formally eliminating the False Positive Problem that SVD-based methods suffer from.

\subsection*{Decoder Fine-Tuning}

The Stable Signature \cite{fernandez2023} embedded the watermark in model weights rather than in the noise vector, by fine-tuning the VAE decoder of a pre-trained latent diffusion model so that every image it decodes carries a fixed binary signature detectable by a paired extractor network. Because the watermark resides in decoder weights rather than image pixels, every output is inherently marked with no inference-time overhead; bit accuracy exceeds 0.99 on 48-bit payloads at PSNR above 38~dB. The fundamental trade-off is access: this method requires control over the generation model and is susceptible to adversarial decoder fine-tuning by an adversary who continues training the weights. PTW (Pivotal Tuning Watermarking) \cite{fernandez2023ptw} addresses this by anchoring the watermark to specific token embeddings through pivotal tuning, making the marked decoder state a stable fixed point that maintains bit accuracy above 0.96 after 500 steps of adversarial weight updates.

\subsection*{Synthesis}

What the diffusion era exposes is a design trilemma. Methods that embed in model weights require model access. Methods that inject structure into the generation process require control over generation. Methods that require neither must accept higher per-image embedding cost or weaker access assumptions. No single method sits at the favourable vertex of all three. Additionally, formal cryptographic soundness and empirical robustness are not the same thing: the PRC Watermark eliminates key-free forgery attacks by reduction to a computationally hard problem, but cryptographic soundness does not automatically translate to robustness against every empirical attack in WAVES. The watermarking literature sometimes lets formal results do more rhetorical work than they deserve.

The shared weakness of the encoder--decoder family --- the one this thesis directly attempts to address --- is that a single monolithic decoder is trained to handle a heterogeneous attack distribution by averaging across all attack types in the loss. This averaging trades per-attack depth for breadth: no single component of the decoder can specialise on the recovery mechanism most appropriate for, say, Gaussian noise versus JPEG compression, because the same weights must serve both. A mixture-of-experts formulation is a natural response to this structure.

\section{Mixture of Experts}

\subsection*{Foundations}

The mixture-of-experts architecture was introduced by \cite{jacobs1991} as a modular approach to supervised learning in which different subsets of the input space are handled by different ``expert'' networks, with a learned gating function determining which expert processes each input. The appeal of the formulation is that it decouples the total model capacity from the per-input computational cost: a system can maintain many specialised sub-networks without applying all of them to every query. \cite{jordan1994} formalised the training dynamics of this system and showed that the gating network and expert networks can be jointly trained by maximising a mixture likelihood, with the expectation-maximisation algorithm providing a natural fitting procedure.

The architecture received relatively little attention through the 1990s and 2000s as deep learning methods grew to dominate the representation learning landscape. The revival came with scale. \cite{shazeer2017} introduced the Sparsely-Gated Mixture-of-Experts layer for language models, demonstrating that a model with over 1,000 expert sub-networks, each activated for only a small fraction of inputs, could achieve substantially lower perplexity than a dense model of equivalent computational cost. The key technical contribution was the top-$k$ routing mechanism: rather than computing a weighted combination of all experts for every input, the gating function selects only the $k$ experts with the highest affinity scores, routing the input exclusively to those $k$ sub-networks and discarding the others. With $k=1$ or $k=2$, the per-example compute budget matches that of a single dense network while the total parameter count scales with the number of experts.

\subsection*{Routing Mechanisms}

The choice of routing mechanism has substantial consequences for training stability. Naive top-$k$ routing without additional constraints suffers from a collapse failure mode: once some experts receive more training signal than others due to random initialisation, the gating function reinforces this imbalance, routing progressively more inputs to the already-advantaged experts until one or two experts handle nearly all inputs and the others receive insufficient gradient to develop useful specialisations. This is routing collapse, and it is the central training challenge for sparse MoE systems \cite{shazeer2017}.

The standard remedies are auxiliary load-balancing losses, which add a differentiable penalty to the training objective that pushes the routing distribution toward uniformity \cite{fedus2022}, and noise injection into the gating logits during training to encourage exploration \cite{shazeer2017}. \cite{lepikhin2020} demonstrated that the load-balancing approach scales to 600 billion parameter models in the GShard framework for neural machine translation. \cite{fedus2022} introduced the Switch Transformer, simplifying to $k=1$ routing and showing that this extreme sparsity is not only stable but often outperforms $k=2$ configurations, because exclusive routing provides each expert with cleaner gradient ownership of its assigned input partition. The $z$-loss regulariser, introduced in the ST-MoE architecture \cite{zoph2022}, penalises large logit magnitudes independently of the load-balancing objective, improving router stability across training.

The distinction between routing collapse (measured by maximum expert load fraction, \texttt{max\_use}) and train--validation routing mismatch (measured by the $L_1$ distance between load distributions on training and validation inputs, \texttt{load\_l1}) is operationally important but rarely made explicit in the literature. A model can exhibit apparently healthy load balance during training while learning a partition that does not transfer to held-out data; the two failure modes are distinct and require separate diagnostic metrics. This distinction is developed further in Chapter~4.

\subsection*{MoE in Vision and Multi-Task Learning}

\cite{riquelme2021} extended the Switch Transformer routing mechanism to vision transformers with V-MoE, replacing alternating dense feed-forward layers with MoE layers and demonstrating that sparse routing provides competitive performance on ImageNet with substantially reduced inference cost. Their buffer token mechanism, which reserves a fixed capacity per expert and routes overflow tokens to a residual path, is one of several approaches to the expert capacity problem: with top-1 routing, the expected input load per expert is $1/N$ of the batch, but variance in routing can cause some experts to be overloaded while others sit idle if no capacity constraint is enforced.

In multi-task learning, \cite{ma2018} demonstrated that a multi-gate mixture-of-experts architecture, in which each task has its own gating network over a shared pool of expert sub-networks, substantially outperforms both shared-bottom and task-specific architectures on tasks with weak inter-task correlation. The relevance to watermarking is direct: different attack types impose structurally different distortions on the watermarked image, analogous to different tasks imposing different objectives on a shared representation. Assigning each attack type to a specialised expert, with a routing function that identifies which distortion the image has undergone, is the exact formulation this thesis explores.

\subsection*{MoE for Robustness Under Distribution Shift}

The robustness motivation for MoE architectures is distinct from the capacity motivation. \cite{guo2018} showed that softmax classifiers are systematically overconfident on out-of-distribution inputs, a problem that modular architectures partially mitigate by restricting each expert's responsibility to the subset of the input space it was trained on. When an input arrives that is genuinely out-of-distribution for all experts, the routing distribution becomes diffuse and the ensemble effectively abstains rather than producing a confident incorrect prediction. For watermark decoding under attack, this property is useful: a router that has learned to associate JPEG-compressed inputs with a JPEG-specialised expert and noise-corrupted inputs with a noise-specialised expert will produce more targeted feature extraction than a monolithic decoder averaging across both.

\cite{jacobs1991}'s original formulation already captures this intuition: the gating function is interpretable as a soft partition of the input space, and the experts are interpretable as local approximators for the function restricted to their assigned region. Training the routing jointly with the experts allows the partition to emerge from the data rather than being specified by hand. Whether this emergence produces the attack-type specialisation that the robustness argument assumes --- rather than some other partition based on image content or style --- is ultimately an empirical question, and one that the routing diagnostics developed in Chapter~3 are designed to answer.

\subsection*{Research Gap}

No prior work has applied a MoE formulation to image watermarking. The closest adjacent work is in multi-degradation image restoration, where \cite{li2022} demonstrated that a MoE framework with degradation-aware routing substantially outperforms single-decoder methods on tasks spanning rain removal, dehazing, and noise reduction, precisely because these degradations require structurally different processing. The parallel to watermark decoding is direct: JPEG compression introduces structured quantisation artefacts in the frequency domain, geometric transforms introduce spatial misalignment, and additive noise introduces unstructured signal corruption. Each requires a different recovery strategy, yet existing encoder-decoder systems assign the same monolithic decoder to all of them. This is not incidental to the HiDDeN design but structural: because the loss averages across attack types, no gradient signal ever pushes any decoder component toward distortion-specific behaviour.
A MoE decoder with learned sparse routing addresses this gap directly. By assigning each input to a specialised expert head, the architecture can maintain dedicated recovery capacity for structurally distinct distortion types without increasing per-image inference cost. The HiDDeN backbone \cite{zhu2018} is a natural starting point because its encoder-decoder architecture cleanly separates the embedding and recovery problems, allowing the MoE component to be isolated to the decoder without altering the encoder or the imperceptibility objective. Chapter 4 characterises the conditions under which this succeeds and the failure modes that arise when they are violated.

\section{MoE Decoder Architecture}
\label{sec:moe-arch}

The MoE decoder replaces the single prediction head of HiDDeN with $N$ parallel expert heads sharing a common feature trunk, plus a learned sparse router that assigns each input to at most $k$ experts. The encoder $E_\theta$ and shared trunk $F_\phi$ are retained from HiDDeN and allowed to adapt during fine-tuning ($\texttt{freeze\_hidden\_backbone}=\mathrm{False}$).

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/moe_decoder_architecture.png}
\caption{End-to-end MoE watermarking architecture on the HiDDeN backbone. The encoder embeds a payload into a cover image; an optional attack or noise channel simulates distortions at evaluation time. The shared trunk $F_\phi$ extracts features that feed a router $W_r$ and $N$ expert prediction heads. Training and primary evaluation both use top-1 sparse routing: one expert decodes each image. A patch discriminator enforces imperceptibility. The five-term objective combines message, image, adversarial, balance, and $z$-loss components; Adam jointly updates the encoder, trunk, router, and experts.}
\label{fig:moe-architecture}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:moe-architecture} summarises the full pipeline; the subsections below define each component mathematically.

\subsection*{Shared Feature Extractor}

The shared trunk extracts a feature vector from the (possibly distorted) watermarked image:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{h} = F_\phi(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_w) \in \mathbb{R}^d,
\end{equation}
where $d$ is the trunk output dimension. $F_\phi$ is the HiDDeN decoder network minus its final linear prediction layer; the router and expert heads receive $\mathbf{h}$ as their shared input. Warm-starting $F_\phi$ from HiDDeN epoch-177 means the trunk already encodes a representation useful for bit recovery, allowing the MoE layer to learn a routing partition over a mature feature space.

\subsection*{Routing Mechanism}

The router maps the feature vector $\mathbf{h}$ to a probability distribution over $N$ experts:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{g} = \mathrm{Softmax}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{W}_r\,\mathbf{h} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}{\tau}\right) \in \Delta^{N-1},
    \label{eq:router}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{W}_r\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}$ are the router weights, $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma_r^2\mathbf{I}_N)$ is exploration noise injected during training only~\citep{shazeer2017}, and $\tau\in(0,\infty)$ is a temperature annealed linearly from $\tau_0$ to $\tau_T$ over training. Lower temperature sharpens the routing distribution toward a one-hot assignment.

Under top-$k$ routing, only the $k$ experts with the highest gate values process each input. For the primary configuration ($k=1$):
\begin{equation}
    k^* = \mathop{\arg\max}_{i\in[N]}\, g_i,
    \label{eq:top1}
\end{equation}
and the selected expert produces the logits:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{z} = \mathbf{W}_{e_{k^*}}\,\mathbf{h} + \mathbf{b}_{e_{k^*}} \in \mathbb{R}^L.
    \label{eq:expert-out}
\end{equation}
At inference with $k=1$, the same hard assignment as training is used: only expert $k^*$ contributes to the logits in Equation~\eqref{eq:expert-out}. The $N-1$ non-selected experts are not used in the decode output, preserving the sparse computational profile that motivates top-$k$ routing. All primary results in this thesis use this hard top-1 decode path.

\subsection*{Expert Networks}

Each expert $e_i$ ($i=1,\ldots,N$) is a linear prediction head:
\begin{equation}
    e_i(\mathbf{h}) = \mathbf{W}_{e_i}\,\mathbf{h} + \mathbf{b}_{e_i}, \quad
    \mathbf{W}_{e_i}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times d},\;\mathbf{b}_{e_i}\in\mathbb{R}^L.
    \label{eq:expert-def}
\end{equation}
All $N$ expert heads are initialised from the HiDDeN decoder's prediction layer weights, so at the start of fine-tuning the MoE decoder is equivalent to an $N$-way ensemble of identical copies of the HiDDeN decoder. Because each training sample updates only the routed expert(s), different experts gradually receive different subsets of the data and specialise for different regions of the feature space. The final decoded bits are:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\mathbf{m}} = \mathrm{round}\bigl(\sigma(\mathbf{z})\bigr),
    \label{eq:decode}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{z}$ is given by Equation~\eqref{eq:expert-out} under top-$k$ routing at both training and evaluation time.

\section{Training Objective}
\label{sec:training-obj}

The MoE decoder introduces two additional regularisation terms to the HiDDeN objective: a balance loss that prevents routing collapse, and a $z$-loss that stabilises router logit magnitudes.

\textbf{Balance loss.} To prevent the router from concentrating all inputs on a single expert~\citep{fedus2022}, a load-balancing auxiliary loss penalises non-uniform routing:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_\mathrm{bal} = \alpha_\mathrm{bal}(t)\cdot N \cdot \sum_{i=1}^{N} f_i\cdot p_i,
    \label{eq:lbal}
\end{equation}
where for a mini-batch of size $B$:
\begin{align}
    f_i &= \frac{1}{B}\sum_{j=1}^{B} \mathbf{1}\!\left[k^{*(j)} = i\right], &
    p_i &= \frac{1}{B}\sum_{j=1}^{B} g_i^{(j)}.
\end{align}
Here $f_i$ is the hard load fraction (non-differentiable) and $p_i$ is the mean soft gate value for expert $i$. Because $f_i$ is non-differentiable, gradients flow only through $p_i$. The product $f_i\cdot p_i$ is large when both hard assignments and soft probabilities concentrate on the same expert, penalising exactly the routing collapse configuration. The weight $\alpha_\mathrm{bal}(t)$ is warmed up linearly from $\alpha_\mathrm{init}$ to $\alpha_\mathrm{final}$ over the first $T_\mathrm{warm}$ epochs to avoid perturbing the warm-started decoder before routing has stabilised.

\textbf{$z$-loss.} The $z$-loss~\citep{zoph2022} penalises large logit magnitudes independently of load balance:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_z = \frac{\lambda_z}{B}\sum_{j=1}^{B}\Bigl(\log\sum_{i=1}^{N}\exp\bigl(g_i^{(j)}\bigr)\Bigr)^{\!2}.
    \label{eq:lz}
\end{equation}
This prevents the router from producing extremely peaked distributions that would make routing brittle to small input perturbations.

\textbf{Total objective.}
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L} = \lambda_\mathrm{msg}\,\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{msg} + \lambda_\mathrm{img}\,\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{img} + \lambda_\mathrm{adv}\,\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{adv} + \mathcal{L}_\mathrm{bal} + \mathcal{L}_z,
    \label{eq:ltotal}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{msg}$, $\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{img}$, and $\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{adv}$ are as defined in Equations~\eqref{eq:lmsg}--\eqref{eq:ladv}. Gradients flow through all differentiable components: the encoder, shared trunk, router weights (via $p_i$ in $\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{bal}$ and directly in $\mathcal{L}_z$), and the selected expert head(s).

\section{Training Algorithm}
\label{sec:training-alg}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:moe-train} gives the complete training procedure. The outer loop iterates over $T$ epochs; inside each epoch the standard mini-batch Adam update is applied to all trainable parameters jointly. Temperature and balance-loss weight are adjusted at the end of each epoch. Routing health is logged on the validation set at each epoch boundary; these diagnostics are used for model selection and early stopping but do not enter the gradient computation.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Train MoE Watermark Decoder}
\label{alg:moe-train}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Pre-trained HiDDeN weights $(E_{\theta_0}, F_{\phi_0}, \mathbf{W}_{e_0}, \mathbf{b}_{e_0})$; training corpus $\mathcal{X}$;
         experts $N$; sparsity $k$; epochs $T$; warm-up $T_\mathrm{warm}$;
         balance weights $(\alpha_\mathrm{init}, \alpha_\mathrm{final})$; $z$-loss weight $\lambda_z$;
         temperature schedule $(\tau_0, \tau_T)$; learning rate $\eta$
\Ensure Fine-tuned encoder $E_\theta$, trunk $F_\phi$, router $\mathbf{W}_r$,
        experts $\{(\mathbf{W}_{e_i}, \mathbf{b}_{e_i})\}_{i=1}^N$
\Statex
\Statex \textbf{Notation:}\; $\mathrm{BCE}(\hat{y},y)=-y\log\hat{y}-(1-y)\log(1-\hat{y})$;\; $\sigma(\cdot)$: sigmoid;\; $[N]=\{1,\ldots,N\}$
\Statex
\Procedure{Train}{}
\State $\theta\leftarrow\theta_0$;\; $\phi\leftarrow\phi_0$;\; $(\mathbf{W}_{e_i},\mathbf{b}_{e_i})\leftarrow(\mathbf{W}_{e_0},\mathbf{b}_{e_0})$ \textbf{for all} $i$ \Comment{warm-start from HiDDeN ep-177}
\State Initialise $\mathbf{W}_r$ with small random weights;\; $\tau\leftarrow\tau_0$;\; $\alpha_\mathrm{bal}\leftarrow\alpha_\mathrm{init}$
\For{$t = 1$ \textbf{to} $T$}
    \For{each mini-batch $\mathcal{B}=\{(\mathbf{x}^{(j)},\mathbf{m}^{(j)})\}$ from $\mathcal{X}$}
        \Statex \hspace{2.5em}\textit{--- Encode ---}
        \State $\mathbf{x}_w^{(j)} \leftarrow E_\theta(\mathbf{x}^{(j)}, \mathbf{m}^{(j)})$ \textbf{for all} $j$ \Comment{no noise layer: clean training}
        \Statex \hspace{2.5em}\textit{--- Route ---}
        \State $\mathbf{h}^{(j)} \leftarrow F_\phi(\mathbf{x}_w^{(j)})$ \textbf{for all} $j$ \Comment{shared trunk}
        \State $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{(j)} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\,\sigma_r^2\mathbf{I}_N)$ \Comment{exploration noise (training only)}
        \State $\mathbf{g}^{(j)} \leftarrow \mathrm{Softmax}\!\left(\left(\mathbf{W}_r\mathbf{h}^{(j)}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{(j)}\right)/\tau\right)$ \Comment{soft routing gates}
        \State $k^{*(j)} \leftarrow \arg\max_{i\in[N]}\,g_i^{(j)}$ \Comment{top-$k$ selection ($k=1$)}
        \Statex \hspace{2.5em}\textit{--- Decode (one expert per image) ---}
        \State $\mathbf{z}^{(j)} \leftarrow \mathbf{W}_{e_{k^*(j)}}\mathbf{h}^{(j)} + \mathbf{b}_{e_{k^*(j)}}$
        \Statex \hspace{2.5em}\textit{--- Losses ---}
        \State $\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{msg} \leftarrow \frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|L}\sum_{j,l}\,\mathrm{BCE}\!\left(\sigma(z_l^{(j)}),\, m_l^{(j)}\right)$
        \State $\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{img} \leftarrow \frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|}\sum_{j}\left\|\mathbf{x}_w^{(j)}-\mathbf{x}^{(j)}\right\|_2^2$
        \State $\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{adv} \leftarrow$ adversarial discriminator loss $\mathcal{D}_\psi$ \Comment{as in HiDDeN}
        \State $f_i \leftarrow \frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|}\sum_{j}\mathbf{1}[k^{*(j)}=i]$;\quad $p_i \leftarrow \frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|}\sum_{j}g_i^{(j)}$ \Comment{load fractions}
        \State $\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{bal} \leftarrow \alpha_\mathrm{bal}\cdot N\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{N} f_i \cdot p_i$ \hfill $\triangleright$ balance loss \eqref{eq:lbal}
        \State $\mathcal{L}_z \leftarrow \frac{\lambda_z}{|\mathcal{B}|}\sum_{j}\!\left(\log\sum_{i=1}^{N}\exp(g_i^{(j)})\right)^{\!2}$ \hfill $\triangleright$ $z$-loss \eqref{eq:lz}
        \State $\mathcal{L} \leftarrow \lambda_\mathrm{msg}\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{msg} + \lambda_\mathrm{img}\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{img} + \lambda_\mathrm{adv}\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{adv} + \mathcal{L}_\mathrm{bal} + \mathcal{L}_z$
        \Statex \hspace{2.5em}\textit{--- Parameter update ---}
        \State $\theta,\;\phi,\;\mathbf{W}_r,\;\{(\mathbf{W}_{e_i},\mathbf{b}_{e_i})\} \;\leftarrow\; \mathrm{Adam}\!\left(\eta,\;\nabla\,\mathcal{L}\right)$
    \EndFor
    \Statex \hspace{1.5em}\textit{--- End-of-epoch scheduling ---}
    \State $\tau \leftarrow \tau_0 - (\tau_0 - \tau_T)\cdot t\,/\,T$ \Comment{linear temperature annealing}
    \If{$t \leq T_\mathrm{warm}$}
        \State $\alpha_\mathrm{bal} \leftarrow \alpha_\mathrm{init} + (\alpha_\mathrm{final}-\alpha_\mathrm{init})\cdot t\,/\,T_\mathrm{warm}$ \Comment{balance-weight warm-up}
    \EndIf
    \Statex \hspace{1.5em}\textit{--- Routing diagnostics (logged, not used in gradient) ---}
    \State $\texttt{max\_use}_t \leftarrow \max_{i}\,f_i^{\mathrm{val}}$ \Comment{routing collapse score; healthy $\approx 1/N$}
    \State $\texttt{load\_l1}_t \leftarrow \left\|\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{train}} - \mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{val}}\right\|_1$ \Comment{routing generalisation gap}
\EndFor
\State \Return $E_\theta,\; F_\phi,\; \mathbf{W}_r,\; \bigl\{(\mathbf{W}_{e_i},\,\mathbf{b}_{e_i})\bigr\}_{i=1}^N$
\EndProcedure
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:eval-protocol}

\subsection*{Performance Metrics}

\textbf{Bit error rate (BER)} is the primary robustness metric:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{BER} = \frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\mathbf{1}\!\left[\hat{m}_l \neq m_l\right].
    \label{eq:ber}
\end{equation}
A BER of 0 indicates perfect recovery; BER of 0.5 is chance performance for a uniformly random 30-bit payload. Bit accuracy is the complement $1-\mathrm{BER}$ and is the form used in plots throughout Chapter~4.

\textbf{PSNR} quantifies embedding imperceptibility:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{PSNR}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}_w) = 10\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{255^2}{\mathrm{MSE}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_w)}\right),
    \label{eq:psnr}
\end{equation}
where MSE is the mean squared error over all pixels. Values above 35~dB are conventionally considered imperceptible.

\textbf{Detection metrics.} For the watermark detection task the decoder output is used to compute a detection score via normalised correlation with the reference payload:
\begin{equation}
    s = \frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\bigl(2\sigma(z_l)-1\bigr)\cdot\bigl(2m_l-1\bigr) \in [-1,1].
    \label{eq:det-score}
\end{equation}
Detection performance is reported as AUROC and TPR at 1\% FPR, computed by treating $s$ as a positive-class score and using unwatermarked images as the negative class.

\subsection*{Attack Suite}

The evaluation applies fourteen image distortions from two complementary suites. The \textbf{HiDDeN noise-layer suite} (nine attacks) uses the original differentiable noise implementations from~\citet{zhu2018}: JPEG compression (DCT keep 25/9/9), 8-bit Fourier quantisation, random crop (keep 55--60\% of H/W), random cropout patch, random pixel dropout, random resize ($0.5$--$1.1\times$, bilinear), additive Gaussian noise ($\sigma\in[0.01,0.05]$ in normalised range), and a combined chain (resize $\to$ JPEG $\to$ crop $\to$ dropout $\to$ Gaussian). The \textbf{WAVES PIL suite} (five additional attacks) uses PIL-based implementations aligned with the WAVES benchmark~\citep{an2024}: rotation ($22.5^\circ$), random resized crop (scale $0.75$), random erasing (12.5\% area), brightness factor ($1.5\times$), and contrast factor ($1.5\times$). All fourteen attacks are applied independently to each watermarked image at evaluation time.

\textbf{MoE decode routing.} For all reported MoE results, decoding uses hard top-1 routing (Equations~\eqref{eq:top1}--\eqref{eq:expert-out}): the router selects a single expert per image and only that expert's logits are decoded. This matches the sparse training objective and the inference-cost analysis in Section~\ref{sec:rq4}. 

\subsection*{Routing Diagnostics}

Two metrics specific to the MoE decoder are computed on validation inputs at each epoch:
\begin{align}
    \texttt{max\_use}  &= \max_{i\in[N]}\; \frac{1}{|\mathcal{X}_\mathrm{val}|}\sum_{j} \mathbf{1}\!\left[k^{*(j)}=i\right], \label{eq:maxuse}\\[4pt]
    \texttt{load\_l1} &= \sum_{i=1}^{N}\left|f_i^{\mathrm{train}} - f_i^{\mathrm{val}}\right|. \label{eq:loadl1}
\end{align}
For $N$ uniformly loaded experts, the expected value of \texttt{max\_use} is $1/N$. Values substantially above this threshold indicate routing collapse. A \texttt{load\_l1} greater than $\approx 0.20$ indicates that the routing partition does not transfer to held-out inputs; the empirical justification for this threshold is developed in Section~\ref{sec:rq5}.

\section{Unified Evaluation Harness}
\label{sec:harness}

To enable reproducible cross-method comparison, every watermarking method evaluated in this thesis is wrapped in a common Python interface exposing three functions: \texttt{embed(image, payload) -> image}, \texttt{detect(image) -> (payload, score)}, and \texttt{config() -> dict}. Attack transforms are applied through a shared pipeline with a deterministic random seed, ensuring identical distortion sequences across methods for any given image index. MoE models decode through hard top-1 routing unless a run is explicitly labelled otherwise. The harness records PSNR, BER, AUROC, and TPR@1\%FPR for every (method, attack, image) triple and writes results to CSV. All results in Chapter~4 are produced by this harness.

\chapter{Experiments and Results}

This chapter evaluates the proposed mixture-of-experts (MoE) watermarking decoder as a design intervention. Sparse expert routing changes the recovery problem: instead of forcing one decoder to reconstruct every payload under every image condition, the model learns a conditional recovery rule in which different inputs are assigned to specialised decoding heads.
The chapter is organised around the design claims required to validate this contribution. Each section identifies the role of a design choice, relates the observed behaviour to the MoE recovery mechanism, and states the implication for the proposed architecture.


\section{Experimental Design and Measures}
\label{sec:setup}

To produce meaningful and conclusive results, the experimental design and evaluation metrics are laid out in the sections below. 

Primary experiments use COCO-2017: 118,287 training images and 5,000 validation images. Each training image receives a random 128 × 128 crop; each validation image uses a fixed deterministic centre crop, so that epoch-to-epoch comparisons reflect model change rather than crop variance. Pixel values are normalised to [−1, 1]. All training and evaluation ran on two NVIDIA T4 GPUs via Kaggle's free-tier notebook environment (CUDA 12.8, PyTorch 2.x). The implementation extends the original HiDDeN repository [14] with three components introduced in this thesis: a sparse MoE decoder, a balance-loss schedule, and routing diagnostics.

The same training loop is used across all experiments; only the design factor under analysis is changed.
The HiDDeN baseline follows its original training protocol on approximately 10k images. R0 uses the full 118k training split for a different reason: under top-1 routing, the corpus is effectively partitioned across four experts, so each specialist receives roughly 29,500 routed examples per epoch at the selected configuration. This is already a tight budget for stable specialisation, as the expert-count ablations in Section 4.3 confirm. Reducing the corpus to match HiDDeN's scale would give each expert approximately 2,500 examples per epoch, a regime the exploratory runs showed is insufficient. The data-scale difference between the two models is therefore a design requirement of the MoE architecture. To produce meaningful and conclusive results, the experimental design and evaluation metrics are defined as follows.

This second metric is essential because a router may look balanced on training data while assigning held-out images according to a different partition. \section{Effect of Sparse Mixture-of-Experts Decoding on Distortion Robustness}
\label{sec:rq1rq2}
%------------------------------------------------------------------

This section evaluates whether sparse top-1 MoE decoding improves distortion robustness relative to HiDDeN while preserving the baseline watermarking objective. Sparse top-1 routing ($k{=}1$) is the reference configuration because it is the only setting that simultaneously supports expert specialisation, matches the sparse inference cost analysed in Section~\ref{sec:rq4}, and aligns training with deployment: each image is routed to one expert at both train and test time (Section~\ref{sec:moe-arch}). A monolithic HiDDeN decoder minimises average-case message loss over the full input distribution; under heterogeneous distortions, that single recovery map must compromise across corruption types that favour different feature-processing strategies. Top-1 routing assigns exclusive gradient ownership to each expert~\citep{fedus2022}, allowing the decoder to partition the input space rather than average over it. Dense routing ($k{=}N$) and top-2 routing increase per-step capacity but share responsibility across experts on every image, which weakens the incentive for stable, transferable specialisation; those settings are retained as ablations in Section~\ref{sec:rq3}, not as the primary model.

The working hypothesis is that a conditionally routed sparse decoder will outperform HiDDeN on distortions that leave spatially local or statistically unstructured residual structure---crop, cropout, Gaussian noise---while remaining competitive on the identity channel and on quantisation. Globally structured transforms such as JPEG and resize are expected to favour the monolithic baseline under clean training, because frequency-domain and scale changes are not naturally factorised by a router trained only on undistorted images. Distortion robustness is evaluated only after identity-channel fidelity is verified: if sparse routing degraded clean BER, encoder MSE, or imperceptibility, any attack gain would confound architectural benefit with objective loss. R0 warm-starts from HiDDeN epoch-177; the identity-channel comparison therefore tests whether the routing layer preserves the pre-trained recovery function before the attack suite measures transfer to corrupted inputs under the same hard top-1 decode rule.

\textbf{Mechanistic interpretation.} The distribution of gains is consistent with the intended role of sparse expert routing. Gaussian noise and crop-like attacks produce local or heterogeneous corruption patterns, so recovery may depend on image-specific cues rather than on a single global inverse rule. A conditional decoder can allocate capacity to different subsets of the input distribution, while a monolithic decoder must average its recovery behaviour across all cases. This result does not establish that each expert corresponds to a human-interpretable corruption class; it supports the narrower claim that sparse routing is beneficial when the corrupted input still contains recoverable structure that varies across images.

\textbf{Boundary case.} JPEG behaves differently. \textbf{Limitation.} The combined attack reduces both models to approximately chance-level bit accuracy for a 30-bit payload. This shared failure is also informative. The proposed MoE decoder is a conditional recovery mechanism inside the HiDDeN paradigm; it does not make clean-channel training sufficient for arbitrary multi-attack robustness.

\textbf{Conclusion.} Sparse top-1 R0 preserves HiDDeN-level clean-channel decoding and improves recovery on several local or spatially heterogeneous distortions, but not on globally structured transforms such as JPEG. The result supports conditional sparse decoding as a targeted robustness mechanism within the HiDDeN paradigm, not as a uniform substitute for the monolithic baseline or for attack-aware training.

\section{Design Rationale for Stable Expert Specialisation}
\label{sec:rq3}
%------------------------------------------------------------------

The R0 configuration is selected by analysing the conditions under which expert specialisation becomes stable. The ablations do not function as a generic hyperparameter search; each one isolates a design pressure that determines whether the MoE layer becomes a routed recovery mechanism or an unstable extension of HiDDeN. The relevant pressures are backbone co-adaptation, routing sparsity, expert count, and training duration.

A mixture-of-experts layer contributes only when expert responsibilities are distinct and transferable. Validation BER alone is insufficient for this purpose, because a model can decode clean images while the router collapses, while gradients are diluted across experts, or while the training partition fails to transfer to validation images. The design analysis therefore identifies the conditions required for stable specialisation.

The most consequential design decision is whether the HiDDeN backbone remains frozen. A frozen backbone appears attractive because it preserves a mature clean-channel representation and restricts learning to the new router and expert heads. However, this assumption conflicts with the purpose of MoE. The original HiDDeN feature space was trained for one monolithic decoder, not for a partitioned set of specialists. If the backbone is frozen, the router must discover stable expert boundaries in a representation that was never shaped to support such boundaries.

When the backbone is unfrozen, the feature extractor, router, and experts can adjust together: as experts begin to own different subsets, the shared representation can become more separable for the router. With a frozen backbone, that feedback loop is broken. A routing-isolation diagnostic run reinforces this interpretation: even explicit pressure to separate experts under a frozen backbone ended with \texttt{max\_use} $=1.00$, meaning that all images were routed to one expert.

Routing sparsity is the second design pressure. The parameter $k$ controls whether an input is assigned to one expert, blended across two experts, or processed by all experts. It is therefore not merely a capacity parameter. It determines whether experts receive exclusive ownership of training examples or whether responsibility is shared.

\section{Computational Consequence of Sparse Expert Dispatch}
\label{sec:rq4}
%------------------------------------------------------------------

The computational analysis evaluates the deployment consequence of replacing one decoder head with a sparsely dispatched set of experts. A four-expert architecture increases stored capacity, but R0 executes only one expert per image. The relevant property is therefore the relationship between expert capacity, dispatch overhead, and batch size.

The computational contribution of the architecture is that it separates stored capacity from executed capacity. R0 stores four expert heads, but top-1 routing activates only one of them per sample. The extra cost should therefore come mainly from the router, masking, conditional dispatch, and less efficient kernel execution, not from evaluating all experts.

The timing protocol measures this distinction directly by separating encoder and decoder latency. The encoder is unchanged between HiDDeN and R0, so any meaningful overhead should appear in the decoder path.

The limitation is batch-1 decoding: when only one image is processed, routing and kernel-launch overhead dominate the measurement, making the current implementation unsuitable for strict real-time single-image verification.

\textbf{Conclusion.} Sparse dispatch makes the additional expert capacity deployable in batched settings, reducing the practical overhead to a moderate decoder penalty rather than a fourfold expert cost. The same mechanism is poorly suited to batch-1 inference unless dispatch is further optimised.

\section{Routing Diagnostics as Predictors of Decoder Failure}
\label{sec:rq5}
%------------------------------------------------------------------

Routing diagnostics constitute a methodological contribution of the thesis. A single-decoder model can often be monitored through validation BER, but an MoE decoder has an internal assignment process that can fail independently of immediate decoding accuracy. The diagnostic analysis establishes why \texttt{max\_use} and \texttt{load\_l1} are necessary for verifying that the model remains a routed decoder.

MoE introduces hidden failure modes. The router can collapse all images to one expert, produce partial imbalance, or appear balanced on training data while routing validation images according to a different distribution. These failures have different causes and require different interpretations, but they may all be obscured if the only reported quantity is final BER.

Within the COCO-100k runs, \texttt{max\_use} correlates with final BER (Pearson $r=0.63$, $p=0.003$ across $n=20$). This separates two failure causes: routing collapse and data-starved underfitting.

The two-dimensional diagnostic plot provides the more important contribution. Full collapse appears as \texttt{max\_use} near one, meaning that the model has effectively returned to a single decoder with unused expert heads. Partial collapse appears when one or two experts dominate the load but the model has not fully degenerated. The third regime is train--validation routing mismatch: \texttt{max\_use} can appear acceptable, while \texttt{load\_l1} becomes large. This case is especially dangerous because it can be missed by standard monitoring. The eight-expert run B illustrates the problem: \texttt{max\_use} is not catastrophic, but \texttt{load\_l1} reaches $1.11$, showing that the validation routing partition is almost unrelated to the training partition.

The diagnostic pair makes the training process interpretable. \texttt{max\_use} measures whether expert capacity is actually used, while \texttt{load\_l1} measures whether the learned assignment structure transfers to held-out inputs. A checkpoint with low clean BER but a widening routing gap no longer represents the intended MoE solution, because the conditional recovery rule has ceased to generalise reliably.

\textbf{Conclusion.} Routing diagnostics are part of the thesis contribution because they identify failure modes that BER alone cannot distinguish. A useful MoE watermarking decoder must be judged by decoding accuracy, expert utilisation, and train--validation routing consistency together.

\section{Positioning Against Classical, Learned, and Diffusion-Native Watermarking Methods}
\label{sec:cross-paradigm}
%------------------------------------------------------------------

The final analysis positions the MoE contribution within the broader watermarking landscape. The objective is interpretive rather than leaderboard-based, because the methods differ in payload type, resolution, detector access, and whether the watermark is embedded after image creation or during generation.

The proposed method modifies a post-hoc learned watermarking pipeline. It is therefore important to distinguish what problem it addresses from problems solved by other paradigms. Classical frequency-domain methods are designed for coefficient-level robustness, self-supervised watermarking uses representation-level invariance, and diffusion-native methods embed provenance information during generation itself.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Classical frequency-domain (DCT).} The Cox et al.~\cite{cox1997} method embeds a spread-spectrum watermark in mid-frequency DCT coefficients. Detection is non-blind and requires the original cover image.
    \item \textbf{Self-supervised post-hoc (SSL Watermark).} Fernandez et al.~\cite{fernandez2022} embed a watermark direction into a frozen DINO feature space. Detection is blind and operates through a linear classifier on the frozen representation.
    \item \textbf{Generation-based (Tree-Ring, ROBIN).} These methods inject watermark structure during diffusion sampling rather than modifying an existing image. Detection is binary rather than bit-level and generally relies on inversion.
\end{itemize}

The comparison shows complementary design strengths rather than a single winner. DCT remains strong on JPEG because its watermark is explicitly embedded in frequency coefficients, but it collapses under crop because the detector depends on spatial alignment with the original cover. Diffusion-native methods are conceptually different again: they are designed for provenance detection during generation and do not carry a multi-bit payload in the same sense as HiDDeN or MoE.

This context clarifies the scope of the thesis contribution. MoE routing does not compete directly with diffusion-native provenance schemes and does not replace representation-level watermarking. It addresses a narrower problem: improving the decoder side of a learned post-hoc bit-recovery system by making recovery conditional on routed expert specialisation. The cross-paradigm evidence suggests that MoE-style conditional decoding is most promising when combined with stronger encoders or attack-aware training, rather than used as a standalone response to every watermarking threat model.

\textbf{Conclusion.} The proposed MoE decoder is best understood as a contribution inside the learned post-hoc watermarking paradigm. It improves conditional bit recovery, but broader robustness against regeneration or highly structured distortions requires complementary mechanisms beyond routing alone.

\chapter{Discussion}
\label{ch:discussion}

This chapter interprets the experimental findings of Chapter~4. The main result is that sparse mixture-of-experts decoding can improve watermark recovery under heterogeneous distortions, but only when the routed model develops stable expert specialisation. The MoE decoder should therefore be understood as a conditional recovery mechanism, not as a universally stronger replacement for HiDDeN.

\section{Answers to the Research Questions}

Returning to the five research questions introduced in Section~1.3, the answers are as follows.

\textbf{RQ1: Does introducing a MoE decoder degrade clean-channel watermarking capability?} No. R0 preserves clean-channel recovery, reaching $0.32\%$ BER compared with HiDDeN's $0.33\%$ BER.

\textbf{RQ2: How does the MoE decoder compare to HiDDeN under image distortions?} It improves robustness on several distortions, but not uniformly across all attacks.

\textbf{RQ3: Which design choices determine whether the MoE architecture converges?} Stable convergence depends on unfrozen co-adaptation, top-1 routing, a manageable number of experts, and stopping before routing drift.

\textbf{RQ4: What is the computational overhead of MoE decoding?} The overhead is large for single-image inference but moderate in batched verification. At batch size 32, the full-pipeline overhead is approximately $1.36\times$.

\textbf{RQ5: Do routing diagnostics predict decoding performance and identify failure modes?} Yes. \texttt{max\_use} and \texttt{load\_l1} expose collapse and train--validation routing mismatch, which BER alone can hide.

These short answers summarise the experimental outcome. The remainder of this chapter explains why these answers hold and what their limits are.

\section{Interpretation of the Main Result}

The main empirical finding is that sparse MoE decoding can improve watermark recovery without destroying the clean-channel capability of the HiDDeN backbone. This matters because a watermark decoder must first solve ordinary payload recovery before robustness under distortion becomes meaningful. R0 satisfies this requirement: its identity-channel BER is essentially identical to HiDDeN's, and its router remains active across all four experts.

The result supports the architectural hypothesis of the thesis. HiDDeN uses one decoder to invert the watermark embedding process for every input. This forces a single set of weights to handle clean images, compressed images, noisy images, resized images, cropped images, and their combinations. The MoE decoder changes the problem by allowing different expert heads to specialise over different regions of the input distribution. Even without attack-specific labels, this conditional structure can provide a better recovery rule than one monolithic decoder.

The evidence should still be interpreted carefully. The experiments do not show that the router learns explicit attack categories such as ``JPEG expert'' or ``noise expert''. Since training is performed only on clean images, the router is more likely learning a partition over image content, embedding structure, or decoder feature geometry. Distorted inputs then inherit this partition imperfectly. The fact that robustness improves under some attacks suggests that the learned partition is useful, but it does not establish a direct semantic mapping between experts and distortion types.

\section{Why Co-Adaptation Matters in This Setting}

The frozen-backbone ablation is one of the most informative results of the thesis. A frozen HiDDeN backbone already contains features that are useful for bit recovery, so it is tempting to treat the MoE decoder as a lightweight replacement for the final prediction layer. The experiment shows that this is not sufficient in the tested configuration. The frozen model does not merely converge more slowly; it develops a large mismatch between training and validation routing distributions.

The likely reason is that the original HiDDeN representation was trained for a monolithic decoder. Its feature geometry only needed to support one global recovery function. A routed decoder requires something different: features that support separable expert responsibilities. When the backbone is unfrozen, the feature extractor can adapt to the router and experts as their roles emerge. Routing decisions shape expert gradients, expert gradients shape the shared representation, and the shared representation becomes more suitable for routing.

This interpretation is intentionally limited. The frozen-versus-unfrozen ablation provides evidence that co-adaptation is important for the tested MoE-HiDDeN configuration, but it does not prove that co-adaptation is a necessary condition for every routed watermarking model. Other designs might use a stronger router, explicit attack labels, contrastive routing losses, or representations pretrained specifically for partitioned decoding.

\section{Expert Ownership and Routing Sparsity}

The routing-sparsity ablation clarifies why top-1 routing is central to R0. Dense routing produces perfect balance by construction, but that balance is not meaningful. If all experts contribute to every image, the model behaves more like a small ensemble than a set of specialists. The router no longer assigns responsibility, and the experts have less reason to differentiate.

Top-1 routing creates expert ownership. Each input produces a full learning signal for one selected head, while the other heads are skipped. This exclusivity encourages each expert to adapt to the subset of images it receives. The $k=2$ case sits between sparse and dense routing: it provides partial specialisation, but responsibility is shared. The continuation run shows that $k=2$ can temporarily produce good clean-channel BER, but its routing partition is less stable.

This result is especially important for watermark decoding because the recovery signal is small and distributed across the image. Dividing that signal across multiple experts can weaken specialisation rather than improve it. In this setting, sparse ownership is more useful than soft participation by many experts.

\section{Data Requirements of Expert Specialisation}

The expert-count ablation shows that adding experts is not automatically beneficial. Eight experts provide more capacity than four, but they also reduce the number of examples routed to each expert. Under top-1 routing, this data-per-expert effect is direct: if the router is balanced, each expert receives approximately $1/N$ of the training examples.

The eight-expert runs did not collapse completely, but they showed worse BER and larger routing gaps than R0. This suggests that the limiting factor was not simply parameter count, but the stability of the learned partition. A larger corpus partially improved BER, but did not remove the routing mismatch. Expert count should therefore be treated as a data-dependent design choice rather than a free scaling dimension.

For this thesis, four experts represent a practical compromise. The model has enough capacity to form multiple recovery heads, but each expert still receives enough routed examples per epoch for stable training. Future work could revisit this trade-off with larger datasets, longer training, or expert-count schedules that grow capacity only after routing has stabilised.

\section{Routing Diagnostics as Scientific Evidence}

A routed model can fail in ways that a monolithic decoder cannot. It can route nearly all inputs to one expert, making the MoE layer effectively useless. It can appear balanced on training data but assign validation images differently. It can also improve clean-channel BER while becoming less robust under attack. For this reason, BER alone is not enough to evaluate an MoE watermark decoder.

The two diagnostics used in this thesis separate these cases. \texttt{max\_use} measures whether one expert dominates the validation set. It detects routing collapse and partial collapse. \texttt{load\_l1} measures whether the load distribution transfers from training to validation. It detects routing partitions that are balanced but not generalisable.

These diagnostics were essential for interpreting the ablations. Several failed configurations would look less problematic if judged only by clean-channel BER. Once routing health is included, their failure becomes clear. This supports the methodological recommendation that future MoE watermarking systems should report routing metrics alongside BER, PSNR, and robustness scores.

\section{Computational Implications}

The computational experiment places a practical boundary around the proposed architecture. R0 stores multiple expert heads, but top-1 routing evaluates only one expert per image. In principle, this avoids the cost of a dense four-expert ensemble. In practice, the implementation still pays for routing, masking, expert selection, and small conditional GPU operations.

The batch-size trend is more important than the raw latency values. At batch size 1, dispatch overhead dominates, making the MoE decoder unattractive for real-time single-image verification. At batch size 32, the overhead is much smaller, and the full-pipeline cost becomes moderate. This means the architecture is better suited to offline or batched verification workflows than to latency-critical interactive use.

The cost result is not a central scientific finding. It does not explain why the MoE decoder improves robustness. Its role is practical: sparse routing avoids dense expert evaluation, but implementation-level dispatch overhead remains important.

\section{Limitations}

Three limitations bound the claims made in this thesis.

First, the frozen-versus-unfrozen comparison is limited to one architecture and one training schedule. The result supports the importance of co-adaptation in the tested MoE-HiDDeN configuration, but it does not establish a universal rule for all routed watermark decoders.

Second, the evaluation uses $128\times128$ COCO crops with a 30-bit payload. This setting is useful for controlled comparison with HiDDeN, but it is not equivalent to deployment-scale watermarking of high-resolution generated images. Scaling to larger images may change the relationship between imperceptibility, payload recovery, and computational cost.

Third, the models are clean-trained. This design isolates whether routing learned on clean images transfers to distorted inputs, but it also limits absolute robustness. Attack-trained HiDDeN and attack-trained MoE models may behave differently. Explicit attack augmentation could strengthen expert specialisation, or it could reduce the need for routing by making the shared representation more robust.

\section{Future Work}

The first direction for future work is attack-aware MoE training. The present thesis intentionally evaluates clean-trained models, but a natural continuation is to expose the model to structured distortions during training and test whether experts align with attack families.

The second direction is improved routing supervision. The router in this thesis is learned only from the recovery objective and auxiliary balance terms. Future work could introduce contrastive routing losses, entropy schedules, or weak attack labels to encourage more interpretable expert partitions.

The third direction is resolution scaling. Watermarking systems intended for generative AI provenance must eventually operate on high-resolution images produced by diffusion models and other large generative systems. The MoE decoder should therefore be evaluated on larger crops, full-resolution images, and diffusion-generated images.

The fourth direction is computational optimisation. The current implementation demonstrates that sparse routing avoids dense expert evaluation, but it is inefficient for batch-1 inference. Kernel fusion, grouped expert execution, or compiling the router and expert dispatch into a single static graph could reduce this latency penalty.

Finally, the routing diagnostics introduced here should be extended. \texttt{max\_use} and \texttt{load\_l1} are useful first-order indicators, but they do not explain what visual or statistical properties define each expert's region. Future work should combine these metrics with expert attribution, feature visualisation, and attack-conditioned routing analysis.

\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{ch:conclusion}

This thesis investigated whether a mixture-of-experts decoder can improve the robustness of learned image watermarking systems. The starting point was a limitation of monolithic encoder--decoder architectures such as HiDDeN: a single decoder must recover the hidden payload across many different image conditions, even though different distortions corrupt the watermark in structurally different ways. JPEG compression, additive noise, resizing, cropping, dropout, and combined attacks do not present the same recovery problem.

The proposed system extends the HiDDeN backbone with a sparse top-1 MoE decoder. A shared feature trunk feeds a learned router and four expert prediction heads. For each image, the router selects one expert during training, giving that expert exclusive responsibility for reconstructing the payload. The model is warm-started from a trained HiDDeN checkpoint and fine-tuned with message recovery, image distortion, adversarial, load-balancing, and router-stabilisation losses.

The primary result is that the selected MoE configuration, R0, preserves clean-channel watermark recovery while improving robustness under several standard distortions. On the identity channel, R0 achieves $0.32\%$ BER, matching HiDDeN at $0.33\%$ BER. This establishes that the routed decoder does not obtain robustness gains by sacrificing basic payload recovery. Routing diagnostics also show that all four experts remain active and that validation routing remains close to training routing.

The ablation study identifies the conditions under which this result holds. Unfreezing the backbone was important because the shared representation, router, and expert heads needed to adapt together. Top-1 routing outperformed blended and dense routing because it created clearer expert ownership. Four experts were more stable than eight under the available data regime. Training beyond the selected checkpoint sometimes improved clean-channel BER but weakened routing stability and attack generalisation.

A methodological contribution of the thesis is the use of routing diagnostics to evaluate MoE watermarking systems. The metrics \texttt{max\_use} and \texttt{load\_l1} expose routing collapse, partial collapse, and train--validation routing mismatch. These failure modes are not visible from BER alone, so future work on routed watermark decoders should report routing-health measures alongside conventional robustness and imperceptibility metrics.

The computational analysis shows that sparse routing avoids the cost profile of a dense four-expert ensemble, but it does not eliminate overhead. At small batch sizes, routing and dispatch dominate latency. At batch size 32, the decoder overhead falls to $1.64\times$, and the full-pipeline overhead is approximately $1.36\times$. The proposed architecture is therefore more suitable for offline or batched verification than for strict real-time single-image inference in its current implementation.

The claims of this thesis remain bounded by the experimental setting. The evaluation uses COCO-2017 crops at $128\times128$ resolution and a 30-bit payload. The models are clean-trained, so the attack results measure transfer from clean routing partitions rather than robustness learned from explicit attack augmentation. The frozen-versus-unfrozen comparison supports the importance of co-adaptation in this configuration, but does not prove that co-adaptation is universally necessary.

Overall, the thesis establishes that sparse expert routing is a viable architectural direction for learned image watermark decoding. It does not solve watermark robustness in general, and it does not remove the known limitations of post-hoc encoder--decoder watermarking under strong generative attacks. However, it shows that the monolithic decoder assumption can be relaxed without destroying clean recovery, and that conditional expert decoding can improve robustness under a heterogeneous distortion suite when routing remains stable.
